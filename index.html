1  What to commit, as a range

Material required by month, in two layers. The grey area is demand from orders already on the books. The orange area is demand from bookings not yet placed. The dotted lines are the range the total is expected to fall within eight times out of ten.

The mix changes across the horizon. Next month is 100% firm; twelve months out it is 28%. That is why the band widens from $10M to $18M, and it is the reason a single confidence level across the whole horizon does not work. The near months can be committed against; the far months are a forecast with a measured error rate.

2  Where the uncertainty comes from

Schedule slip from booked date to delivery, by market, across 848 orders.

The market-level structure here was specified when the data was generated, so this is a recovery test: the question is whether market-level aggregation recovers a structure that a regional average destroys. India is carried separately because its distribution sits well outside the others, at a P90 of 18.5 months against 1.5 for US and Canada.

3  Why the regional average is the wrong unit

The same P90s, grouped by region, with the blended regional figure marked. In the two regions that contain more than one market, the regional number describes neither half of it.

APAC blends to 10.8 months across Australia and South East Asia at 4.1 and India at 18.5, with India at 44% of APAC bookings. EMEA blends to 4.7 across Europe at 4.0 and MENA at 5.5. A plan set on either regional figure runs early on one market and late on the other.

4  The same requirement, split by region

Forward material requirement by region, with India dotted inside the APAC panel. Each region is on its own scale, because North America is roughly 69% of volume and flattens the other three when they share an axis. Compare shape and band width across panels, not height.

Quantiles do not add. Summing the four regional P90s gives a figure 15.9% above the portfolio P90, because the regions do not all run late in the same month. The difference is material that would be bought and not needed. The size of that gap depends on an assumption worth stating: slip is drawn independently per order, with no shared shock. Real slip correlates through tariffs, interconnection queues and financing conditions, and under positive correlation this benefit shrinks. A production model would estimate that correlation rather than assume it away.

5  Realization rate as a control

Material committed against open backlog at a given realization assumption. Move the slider.

The distance between the ends of the scale is the exposure the assumption controls. Setting it at 100% commits steel against projects that slip; setting it low risks dates on the projects that hold.

6  Was the forecast right, and by how much

Conversion lookback. What was expected to convert each month against what actually did, by region, with cumulative bias.

A region with persistent bias is one where the realization rate can be measured rather than chosen. Cumulative bias is the quantity that sets it, and it is also what downstream functions are already correcting for informally.

The same question asked of the interval itself. Standing at each of 8 past month-ends, forecasting forward using only what was known then, and checking how often the outcome fell inside the stated range.

Across 48 month-forecasts the stated 80% interval contained the outcome 88% of the time, and the stated 50% interval 69%. 4 misses fell above the range and 2 below. The interval is wider than it needs to be rather than overconfident, which is the safer direction but still means committed material is being held against uncertainty that is not there.

The near months are the weak ones. Month +1 covers 62% against 100% further out, which is the opposite of what most people expect and is worth knowing before anyone commits against month one.

No correction is applied to these intervals, deliberately. Adjusting an interval to hit its stated coverage is a standard technique and I built it, then took it out. 8 cutoffs over six months produce 48 observations but only 13 distinct outcome months, because overlapping windows score the same month repeatedly. Split across six forecast horizons that leaves about five points each, and the arithmetic needs at least six to construct an 80% bound at all, so what comes out is the range of five points wearing the label. It would have looked like method and meant nothing. Roughly two years of retained forecasts is the point at which it starts to, which is the same prerequisite as everything else on this page.

What this needs to run

None of it works without a record of expected conversion dates. Each order needs the date it was expected to convert, recorded when that date was set, and kept when the date moves. The lookback measures against that record.

As you described the current process, those dates already exist in the pipeline and get overwritten each month rather than retained, so there is nothing to measure against. If that is right, keeping them costs very little and has to come before any modeling work.

Without the record, the realization rate is a number someone picks and the interval has no tested coverage. Each function downstream then applies its own unstated discount to the plan. Measuring bias puts that correction in one place, where all four are working from the same number.
