1  What to commit, as a range

Median material requirement by month across the open order book, with an 80% interval. The outer band is the same interval after conformal calibration widened it to match observed accuracy.

The width of the band is the safety margin implied by the current order book. It is the quantity a commitment decision is actually made against.

2  Where the uncertainty comes from

Schedule slip from booked date to delivery, by market, across 895 orders.

India is tracked separately because its distribution sits well outside the others, at a P90 of 9.8 months against 1.8 for US and Canada.

2b  Why the regional average is the wrong unit

The same P90s, grouped by region, with the blended regional figure marked. In the two regions that contain more than one market, the regional number describes neither half of it.

APAC blends to 6.0 months across Australia and South East Asia at 4.1 and India at 9.8, with India at 63% of APAC bookings. EMEA blends to 4.7 across Europe at 3.4 and MENA at 7.4. A plan set on either regional figure runs early on one market and late on the other.

2c  The same requirement, split by region

Forward material requirement by region, with India shown dotted inside APAC.

Quantiles do not add. Summing the four regional P90s gives a figure about 15% above the portfolio P90, because the regions do not all run late in the same month. The difference is material that would be bought and not needed.

3  Realization rate as a control

Material committed against open backlog at a given realization assumption. Move the slider.

The distance between the ends of the scale is the exposure the assumption controls. Setting it at 100% commits steel against projects that slip; setting it low risks dates on the projects that hold.

4  Was the forecast right, and by how much

Conversion lookback. What was expected to convert each month against what actually did, by region, with cumulative bias.

A region with persistent bias is one where the realization rate can be measured rather than chosen. Cumulative bias is the quantity that sets it, and it is also what downstream functions are already correcting for informally.

Walk-forward test across 48 month-forecasts. The raw model was overconfident: a stated 80% interval contained the outcome 62% of the time. Conformal calibration measured the gap and widened the interval by 1.53x, bringing realised coverage to 83%.

What this needs to run

None of it works without a record of expected conversion dates. Each order needs the date it was expected to convert, recorded when that date was set, and kept when the date moves. The lookback measures against that record.

Those dates already exist in the pipeline. They get overwritten each month instead of retained, so there is nothing to measure against. Keeping them costs very little and has to come before any modeling work.

Without the record, the realization rate is a number someone picks and the interval has no tested coverage. Each function downstream then applies its own unstated discount to the plan. Measuring bias puts that correction in one place, where all four are working from the same number.
